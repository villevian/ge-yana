Is the bystander effect a myth?

An adapted article · based on Philpot et al., American Psychologist (2019)

Remember the article in lesson 1B? A man was lying on the floor of a crowded bus, and nobody helped. Psychologists call this the bystander effect: the more people are around, the less likely any one of them is to help, because everyone thinks somebody else will do it.

The idea became famous after a crime in New York in 1964, when newspapers reported that dozens of neighbours heard a woman’s cries and did nothing. For decades, the story was in psychology textbooks. But later research showed that the newspapers had exaggerated: some neighbours did try to help — for example, by calling the police.

In 2019, a team led by Richard Philpot of Lancaster University decided to look at real life instead of laboratory experiments. They watched 219 CCTV videos of arguments and fights in the streets of Lancaster (UK), Amsterdam (the Netherlands) and Cape Town (South Africa). What they found was surprising: in nine out of ten conflicts, at least one bystander — and usually several — stepped in to help. They calmed people down, pulled them apart or comforted the victim.

Even more surprisingly, the more people there were, the more likely it was that someone would help. And the results were almost the same in all three cities, including Cape Town, which has a much higher crime rate.

So should we forget the bystander effect? Not completely. One person alone may still hesitate in a crowd. But the good news is clear: if you are ever in trouble in a public place, the chances are high that a stranger won’t let you down.
